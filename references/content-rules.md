# Content & Writing-Standard Rules

These are substantive writing rules — they shape what the resume *says*, independent of how it's
laid out on the page. They come from the Smith School of Business Office of Career Services (OCS)
resume guidelines. Apply them to every bullet and line of text you write or edit, regardless of
which visual template is in play.

## Don't silently drop or compress the candidate's content

When transcribing what a candidate tells you (or an existing resume they paste in) into the
`resume_data.json`, preserve every substantive detail they gave you — project names, course codes,
tools, scope, numbers. You're allowed, and expected, to tighten *wording* (action verb, grammar,
SAR structure per the rest of this file), but that's different from cutting *content* to make
something fit. If a section genuinely won't fit on one page, say so explicitly and ask the
candidate what to cut — don't quietly shorten or merge their bullets/details to make the problem
disappear. A resume that silently lost a project's context line or collapsed two distinct
accomplishments into one is a worse bug than a resume that's honestly a line too long.

## Bullet structure (SAR, results-based)

- Every bullet starts with a **past-tense action verb** (see `action-verbs.md`). Use present tense
  only for something the candidate is still actively doing right now (e.g., a current TA role).
- A bullet is a results-based accomplishment, not a responsibility listing. Build it on **SAR**:
  Situation (the context/problem), Action (what the candidate specifically did), Result (the
  quantified outcome or benefit to the organization). See `bullet-writing-framework.md` for the
  full elicitation method and worked examples — read that file before drafting any new bullet.
- Quantify wherever a number is available: time saved, revenue generated, costs reduced, people
  trained/managed, error rates, adoption rates, etc. State facts ("20% increase," "over 5,000
  customers") rather than judgments ("a big increase," "a large customer base").
- Each bullet should reveal something *different*. Don't repeat the same accomplishment across two
  jobs. If someone did genuinely progress in a repeated duty across roles, show the progression
  instead of restating it.
- Order bullets within a block by relevance/value to the reader, most important first.

## Grammar and punctuation

- No possessives (my, I, our) and no contractions (it's, didn't, wouldn't, don't).
- Limit articles ("the," "a," "an") — resume language is clipped, not full prose.
- Spell out "and" — never use "&".
- End every bullet and every strategic-impact statement with a period.
- `cum laude`, `summa cum laude`, `magna cum laude` are always italicized and lowercase.
- Hyphenate compound modifiers: "first-year," "second-year," "full-time."

## Abbreviations and acronyms

- Avoid abbreviations in general. The one standing exception is state/country abbreviations in a
  location line, e.g. "College Park, MD, USA."
- Spell out degree names: "Master of Science" rather than "MS."
- Spell out an acronym the first time it's used, then use the short form after: "Department of
  Defense (DoD)," "Strengths, Weaknesses, Opportunities, Threats (SWOT) analysis." **GPA and GMAT
  are exceptions** — they can be used unspelled throughout.

## Numbers

- Spell out one through nine: "led team of three."
- Use digits for 10 and above: "led team of 12."
- Exception that always wins regardless of magnitude: money and percentages are always digits —
  "$2B in revenue," "5% growth," "$250K."

## Section-by-section content guidance

- **Education** — reverse chronological, current degree first. Never list high school. Include
  GPA only if 3.5 or above, GMAT only if 650 or above (defaults — override if the candidate
  explicitly wants it shown anyway). May include scholarships, relevant coursework, case
  competitions, and leadership/membership in student organizations.
- **Technical Skills** — certifications, tools, programming languages, and operating systems the
  candidate has real proficiency in (through employment or academic work), not just passing
  familiarity.
- **Project Experience** — use this section when the candidate doesn't yet have professional
  experience that demonstrates a target skill, or is transitioning careers and needs a vehicle to
  show a transferable skill. Classroom projects and case-competition entries both qualify. Name the
  technology/methods used and the strategic impact — not just "what the project was about."
- **Work Experience** — reverse chronological, one block per employer. If someone held multiple
  titles at one employer, use a single employer header with stacked title/date sub-blocks rather
  than repeating the employer. Starts after the candidate's undergraduate degree — skip high-school
  jobs unless they're genuinely relevant evidence of business skill.
- **Leadership/Volunteer or "Additional Experience and Leadership"** — this is where well-roundedness
  shows up, and it matters more for candidates with less professional work experience. Keep entries
  compact (role + org + one or two bullets), not full job blocks.
- **Distinctions** — languages, publications, additional training, volunteer work, hobbies,
  interests, awards. Short one-line entries.

## Name / contact block

- Name, phone, email, LinkedIn URL are the required fields.
- An optional third line can carry work authorization or a security clearance, e.g. "Eligible to
  work in the U.S. under F-1 OPT" or "Permanent U.S. Work Authorization."
- Never include: visa requirements beyond a simple authorization statement, a Social Security
  number, marital status, or a photo.

## Length

- One page. Always. If a draft doesn't fit, cut the least-relevant bullet or tighten language
  before shrinking fonts or margins below the house spec in `references/visual-spec.md`.
