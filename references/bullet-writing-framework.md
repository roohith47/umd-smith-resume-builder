# Bullet-Writing Framework

Use this method every time you draft a new bullet from scratch, or rewrite a weak one. It comes
from the Smith OCS "Creating Impactful Resume Bullet Points" coaching material.

## Forward-looking, not backward-looking

A resume is a marketing tool aimed at the *next* job, not an autobiography of everything the
candidate has ever done. A **backward-looking** resume lists accomplishments regardless of
relevance and leaves the reader to figure out why any of it matters. A **forward-looking** resume
is built around the transferable skills the target role actually needs.

When the candidate has a specific job or target role in mind:

1. Pull up roughly three real job descriptions for that role (or ask the candidate what the
   posting emphasizes if you don't have the text).
2. Note the skills that repeat across the responsibilities/qualifications sections.
3. Check which of those skills the candidate's actual experience already demonstrates.
4. Write bullets that foreground that overlap — lead with the skill the target employer is
   screening for, using the candidate's real experience as the vehicle.

If there's no specific target role yet, write the strongest general-purpose version of each bullet
and flag that it's worth revisiting once a target posting exists.

## The three-stage strengthening pattern

Every bullet can be pressure-tested by asking "is this just a responsibility, or does it show a
result, or does it show a result *and* how I achieved it?" Each stage is strictly better than the
last:

**Stage 1 — Responsibility only (weak):**
> Coach 400 MBA and MS students every year.

**Stage 2 — + Result (better):**
> Coach 400 MBA and MS students every year, resulting in a 90% employment outcome rate.

**Stage 3 — + Relevant skill / how (best):**
> Coach 400 MBA and MS students every year by providing one-on-one coaching, including resume
> writing, value proposition creation and offer negotiations, resulting in a 90% employment
> outcome rate.

A second worked example, starting from a bare task:

> Automated a manual process
> → Automated a manual process, saving the company $X.
> → Used Python to automate a manual process, saving the company $X.

Note how the final version names the *tool* (transferable skill) alongside the *result* — that's
the combination that makes a bullet land for both a human reader and an applicant tracking system
matching against a job description's required skills.

## Eliciting a bullet via SAR

When drafting a bullet from scratch, ask the candidate (or infer from what they've told you):

- **Situation** — What was the problem, context, or conflict going in?
- **Action** — What did *they* specifically do? (Not "the team" — their action.)
- **Result** — What changed because of it? Quantify if at all possible. Who benefited
  (organization, client, department) and how?

Also note the **transferable skill** the bullet demonstrates (e.g., problem solving, stakeholder
influence, data analysis, negotiation). This is what lets you re-weight which bullets appear, and
in what order, when tailoring the same experience toward two different target roles.

Once you have Situation/Action/Result, compress it into one bullet (2–3 lines max): lead with the
action verb, fold in the action and the relevant skill, close with the quantified result. Don't try
to cram the entire SAR story into the bullet — the full narrative is what the candidate tells in an
interview; the bullet is the headline that earns the interview.

## Quick self-check before finalizing a bullet

- Does it start with a past-tense action verb?
- Does it name *how* the result was achieved (a tool, method, or skill), not just the outcome?
- Is there a number in it, if any number is available?
- Would this bullet make a recruiter screening for the target role's listed skills stop and read
  the rest of the resume?
- Is it free of possessives, contractions, and the ampersand (see `content-rules.md`)?
